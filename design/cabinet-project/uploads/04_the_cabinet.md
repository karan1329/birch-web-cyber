# The Cabinet · a design language for Birchlogic

---

## Part 1 · The thesis

The reference image works because it deflates something. A cloud is sold as ethereal, weightless, infinite. The picture says: it is a computer. A boxy beige machine on a desk. The sublime thing turns out to be mundane, and the viewer feels a small click of recognition.

Security is sold the same way. Threat landscape. Attack surface. Posture. Zero trust. Every word in the category is built to sound like weather.

**Our deflation: security is a filing problem.**

Not a fortress problem, not a war problem, not a wizard problem. When a regulator writes to you, when an enterprise customer's security team blocks a deal, when an investor's technical diligence lands, not one of them is asking whether you are safe. Every one of them is asking a filing question: *where is the document, who owns it, when was it last true, and does it match what you told us.*

That is what a firm gets wrong. Not the firewall. The filing.

**The line that carries it:** everything they are going to ask you is already in a drawer. The only question is whether anyone has opened it.

That is the CRT-in-the-cloud move, made ours. It is contrarian, it is true, and it is exactly the business you actually run. You do not install firewalls. You read, sort, score, and answer.

## Part 2 · The object

**The Cabinet.** A run of flat-file drawers. The word carries two meanings and both are wanted: a piece of furniture that holds records, and a body that governs.

It is not a filing cabinet in the sad-office sense. It is closer to a map cabinet, an archive drawer, a plan chest. Wide, shallow, heavy, well made. The kind of furniture that implies an institution rather than a startup.

### The anatomy is a meaning map

Every part of the object corresponds to a part of the business. This is what stops it being decoration.

| part | what it is | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| **the face** | the closed drawer front, a label holder, a pull | what the outside world sees. The surface a regulator, an auditor or a customer meets |
| **the drawer, open** | pulled out on its runners | the Read. The moment of *show me* |
| **the tabs** | raised card tabs across the top | your taxonomy. IAM, GRC, App Sec, Cloud, Vuln Mgmt, Third Party |
| **the cards** | standing files inside | the twenty-two controls, each with its evidence behind it |
| **the empty slot** | a gap in an otherwise full drawer | **the finding** |
| **the redaction bars** | solid black on a visible card | what comes out before it goes onward |
| **the back of the drawer** | receding into shadow and noise | the machinery. Firewalls, EDR, SIEM, identity, cloud config, scanners |
| **the runners** | the rails the drawer travels on | the three weeks. Day 11 is full extension |
| **the wall of cabinets** | closed, labelled, quiet | the Security Office, working |

### The empty slot is the whole product

Take one thing from this document and take this. In a full, ordered, well made drawer, **a single missing card is the most legible thing in the frame.** The eye finds it before it finds anything else.

That is your entire pitch, rendered without a word. We open the drawer and show you the empty slot, while there is still time to fill it, before somebody with power opens the same drawer and finds it themselves.

No padlock, no shield, no glowing perimeter, and infinitely more specific than any of them.

## Part 3 · The central mechanic

The user's own framing is the key: **in front, the compliance questions and checklists. Behind, the tools, the networks, the firewalls, all taken care of.**

So the drawer is not a container. It is a **transformer**. Disorder goes in the back. Ordered answers come out the front. Birchlogic is the mechanism in between.

Render that as a single visual law:

> **Dither is entropy. Line is order. The drawer is where one becomes the other.**

The further back into the cabinet, the coarser the dithering, until the machinery is only ghost shapes made of dots: a rack, a switch, an appliance, a cable run, half-legible. Coming forward, the dots resolve into clean single-weight line, then into crisp type on a card, then into a printed tab you can read from across the room.

This is not a texture choice. It is an argument you can see. It also means every image on the site obeys one rule, which is the thing both reference shots do and the thing that makes a design language rather than a set of pictures.

It also solves a practical problem. Machinery drawn in full detail is ugly and dates instantly. Machinery drawn as dithered ghost forms is timeless, cheap, and says *this is handled, you do not need to look at it* — which is precisely the offer.

## Part 4 · The five scenes

The language is not one hero image. It is five compositions of one object, and together they carry the argument down the page.

### Scene 1 · The hero. *The drawer, open.*

Three-quarter isometric. One drawer pulled from a cabinet that continues out of frame on all sides, so the object is implied to be much larger than what you see.

Front two thirds: crisp line. Tabs upright and readable. Cards standing in order.
Back third: dissolving into dither, and inside the dither the faint shapes of the machinery.
Near the front, **one empty slot**. Lying across the open drawer, the card that belongs in it, face up, with three redaction bars on it.

Everything the page needs to say, in one frame.

### Scene 2 · The problem. *What arrives at the face.*

Flat elevation, dead on, no perspective. The closed cabinet face. Leaning and stacked against it: a regulator's letter, a customer security questionnaire, an auditor's request list, an investor's diligence pack. All dithered, all slightly askew, none of them sorted.

The face itself is clean line. The pile is noise. The contrast is the problem statement.

### Scene 3 · The index. *Twenty-two slots.*

Top-down plan view of the drawer interior. A grid of slots. Each holds a card carrying its score, zero to four, set in mono. Several slots empty.

This is the Security Read Index, rendered as furniture instead of as a chart. Identical information, but it is a *thing* rather than a dashboard, which is exactly the register a technical buyer trusts more.

### Scene 4 · The three weeks. *The runners.*

Section view. The rails the drawer rides on, with four positions marked along the travel. Days 1 to 3 barely out. Days 4 to 10 half. **Day 11 at full extension, everything visible.** Days 12 to 15 closing again with the card now in its slot.

The process drawn as a mechanism rather than as a timeline with four dots.

### Scene 5 · The close. *The wall.*

Wide, distant, quiet. A full wall of cabinets receding, every drawer closed, every label filled, nothing happening.

This is what the Security Office looks like when it is working. It is the calm end state, and it is the emotional payoff the page has been earning since the first frame. Nothing is on fire. Everything is filed.

## Part 5 · The system that falls out of it

The object generates the interface. That is the test of a real design language.

**Tabs become section markers.** A raised tab shape with a mono uppercase label is the page's section band. It already exists in the object, so it is not a borrowed device.

**Cards become content blocks.** Every panel on the page is a card in a slot: same proportion, same hairline edge, same tab position.

**The slot grid becomes the layout grid.** Slot pitch sets the column rhythm. The drawer's interior proportion sets the card aspect.

**The empty slot becomes the null state.** Anywhere the site has nothing to show, it shows a slot with no card in it, labelled. Never a spinner, never a shrug.

**Redaction becomes the emphasis mark.** Solid black bars, hard edged, the only pure black in the system. Used sparingly, they are unmistakably yours.

**The runner becomes the progress rail.** Scroll progress, form steps, the three-week timeline, all the same rail.

**Colour follows the depth law.** Navy `#0E1B2C` on bone `#F4EFE6`. Ochre `#B8862F` reserved for exactly one thing: **the tab of the drawer currently open, and the empty slot.** One accent, and it always means *look here, this is the live one*. That is a rule with a job, not a decoration.

## Part 6 · Motion grammar

One mechanism, used consistently, never for flourish.

**Things open.** The hero drawer slides out on load, once, 620ms, decelerating like something with mass. It does not bounce.

**Cards rise on arrival.** Section content enters as a card rising into its slot, 16px, 620ms, staggered 70ms.

**Redaction is a hard cut.** The bars do not fade. They land, 60ms apart, after everything else has settled. No easing. It should feel like a stamp.

**The rail fills as you read.** Scroll progress travels the runner. It is the only thing on the page that tracks the reader, and it is the only motion that is genuinely functional.

**Under `prefers-reduced-motion`,** the drawer renders already open, the cards already seated, the bars already down. The composition was designed as a still first, so nothing is lost.

## Part 7 · What the language refuses

Refusals are positioning, and this language has strong ones.

No padlocks, shields, hoodies, matrix rain, circuit boards, hexagons, glowing perimeters, attack maps, or any figure at a screen. The Cabinet exists so none of these are ever needed.

No photography of people. The object carries the human implication by being *used*: a card pulled, a slot open, a tab worn. Nobody needs to be in frame.

No second accent colour. Ochre means open. Nothing else gets a colour.

No skeuomorphic wood grain, brass fittings, or vintage patina. The cabinet is drawn, not rendered. Single weight line, dither, type. The moment it looks like a stock photo of an antique it stops being an argument and becomes a mood.

No drawer that is chaotic inside. Ever. The whole claim is that we make it orderly. Disorder lives only in the dithered back and in the pile at the face.

## Part 8 · How it gets made

**Draw it, do not generate it.** True isometric SVG at thirty degrees, single stroke weight, no fill, no shading. Then run the dither as a post process over the render, with dot density driven by depth.

This buys three things a generated image cannot give you. Correct perspective, which was the one real failure in the crimson version. Editable tabs, so the taxonomy can change without regenerating anything. And individually addressable cards, so the whole thing animates.

One master file, five camera positions, five scenes. The object is built once.

**Dither spec.** Atkinson, navy on bone, no greys. Dot size increases with depth: fine at the drawer face, coarse at the back. That gradient is the argument, so it is the one parameter worth tuning carefully.

---

## Part 9 · Build order

1. **The isometric drawer in SVG.** One drawer, correct perspective, tabs as live text, cards as addressable groups, one empty slot. Everything else derives from this file.
2. **The depth dither pass.** Get the entropy-to-order gradient right at hero scale before drawing any other scene.
3. **Scene 1 only,** placed in the page, judged against the real type at real size.
4. Scenes 3 and 5 next, because the Index and the Office are the two things you are actually selling.
5. Scenes 2 and 4 last. They are supporting argument, and by then the language will be settled enough that they take an hour each.

## The sentence to keep

Everything a regulator, an auditor, or an enterprise customer is going to ask you is already in a drawer. We open it first, we find the empty slot, and we fill it before anyone with power comes looking.
